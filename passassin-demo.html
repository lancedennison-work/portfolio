Project Goal

The aim of this project is to make an ambitious Unreal Third-Person Stealth-Shooter game from the ground up, only using blueprint nodes where C++ fails to be useful (like ABP graphs). I am going to go off my first guess to create novel (to me) solutions for the first iterations of the classes, and refine the technical elements towards my goal designs or game-design-standard solutions I remember.

Pitch

Inspirations

I have a few different games that will guide the direction of my design goals.

Stealth: Dishonored

I want a complex stealth system: Enemies that—

  • Can patrol
  • Have a vision cone
  • Vision state that builds a tolerance value up to spotting the character
    • And back down if not triggered
  • Attack when able
  • Investigate when vision fully gained, then lost
  • Goes back to patrol when found nothing
  • Alerted mode when someone raises the alarm

dishonored enemy detection

Enemy Behavior: Halo

I want flavorful enemy behaviors; which the Halo franchise famously has. Each enemy type has so much personality baked into them through their behavior tree. This is well supported by the amazing animation of each "class" that sells the experiential narrative that—the Covenant as a prime example—are distinct races in a strict caste system socially and militarily.

covenant behavior

Group Behavior: The Last of Us

I want a system to allow for cooperative enemy behavior—like how TLoU enemies can "bark" to others, work together to locate you, and otherwise act like people who use the numbers they have as an advantage. This differs from many stealth games which, a lot of the time, seem to have you almost magically globally visible once the alarm has been raised. With a more nuanced system, like TLoU, actors have to share information for them to accurately pinpoint your location.

Player

Functional Crosshair

The—key, new—feature that the PlayerAgent needs—that isn't cut out from the pre-split Agent class, is a crosshair/reticle. Currently the way the AAgent shoots: it draws a trace hitscan out using the muzzle as the start of the vector, then it travels (firing range) down the camera forward vector to get the trace end, and hits anything along that new path. In third person games, there is always the issue of the camera and the gun being in very different places, and the closer an object is to the gun, the more extreme that difference becomes. So, you have to find how to balance between the bullets—at least feeling like they are—coming out of the gun, and going to where you are aiming.

Sometimes this means they show you where you are aiming, and where you are going to hit, like in Helldivers 2:

helldivers 2 — separate aim and hit indicators

To test my options and find how I want the hitscan-behavior/reticle-placement to function, I needed a way to probe in the same direction relative to the player's weapon every tick.

Manually updating the direction based on the player's movement and rotation every tick, to test for collisions, seemed excessive. Especially, when the camera boom does exactly what I am looking for.

The camera boom uses a Spring Arm Component: it has a fixed path, with a maximum range, and changes its length depending on physical collision; it's just normally supposed to be behind the player. So, I socketed one to the weapon muzzle, and one to the follow camera and flipped them around.

Voilà.

muzzle probe, camera probe, and the sweep between them

This perfectly creates a no-fuss, consistent angle, with linear probing at fixed maximum distance, every tick. Now I can see the path of the gun, where it is hitting and where the camera is hitting (it's always pointing at the center of the screen). I then added a sweeping line between the end probe sockets of the arms (where they are colliding with something), and if an Agent collides with that path, I get a blended target vector for the pseudo-midpoint.

To test which one I liked, I chose one as the winner, each tick: whichever collision was hitting the closest agent, is the one that the gun used for Fire(). That way, I could test all three options and together as a fourth, secret, option.

all three hitscan debugs at once

The 3 options.

  • Red: Trace from muzzle to Camera
    • Pros: Highest accuracy; its target location doesn't bob around from the idle animation, and is always at the center of the screen so you don't have to coax the camera into lining up the hitscan
    • Cons: Looks weird if you can see the line the closer you are to the target as it skews further away from the barrel direction, and when you get the barrel close enough to something it kind of breaks.
    • Accurate past close range for both hipfire and aiming
  • Blue: Trace from muzzle straight out
    • Pros: Works well up close, and is animation-accurate.
    • Cons: Moves around a lot the farther away you are aiming due to aforementioned accuracy with the idle animation. Primitive rotational movement and no IK on the arms means the trace end is almost never at the center of the screen.
    • Accurate at very close range, more accurate during aiming due to camera offset.
  • Purple: Trace sweep between arms
    • Pros: Makes up for the inconsistent distance weaknesses of the bounding arms.
    • Cons: Though it works well to balance at long range since the angle gets very close to the red, it does not equally balance the middle distances
    • Maybe the least accurate at this stage, very hard to balance just right.

The real solution is animation / moving the gun to point in the direction of the "Red" option. But this was a fun test to set up.

Enemy

Behaviors

Currently, enemies have four behavior tree states: Patrol, Investigate, Attack, and Die. While patrolling, they move towards their next patrol point, then to the next once they reach it. When investigating, they go to the last place they saw the player, if they can no longer see them. When attacking, they face the player and shoot. When they die, well… they're dead.

enemy behavior state loop

Behavior Modifiers

Alarmed is a behavior condition; the reason it is not a primary state is because it alters their global behavior, an enemy can be alarmed in any of previously mentioned states.

What it does

When an enemy is Alarmed, they are actively looking, instead of passively guarding. Instead of just keeping an eye out on their "post" (close surroundings), they are searching for an enemy that isn't close to them since someone else spotted them.

Additionally, when on alarm, everyone knows that something is going on, so the enemies will have greater tolerances for searching when alarmed.

Why?

This provides a more realistic sentry behavior. If you are guarding the back of a building, but someone near the front raises the alarm, you are going to search a much more distant area, requiring you to focus, tightening your (active) field of vision as you strain to look farther away. Of course, you aren't a camera, you aren't zooming in; but you are switching from a passive scan to a focused search, so your brain will shift priority from your peripheral vision. Also, if someone had raised the alarm, you are going to look around for a lot longer than a "hey what was that" search.

More Realistic Detection

If you are in the center of someone's vision, you are going to be spotted far quicker than if you are on the peripherals of someone's vision, so the cone needs to build up at a different rate depending on the angle.

I have three options I intend to use for different behavior profiles.

linear (red), ramping (purple), and threshold (green) fill curves

  • Red: Linear detection, detection rises at the same rate as the angle approaches the center
  • Purple: Ramping detection, detection increases at a proportionally higher rate closer to the center
  • Green: Detection thresholds, detection stays at a set rate until within the next section of the cone, then it jumps to that section's set rate.

I am going to use the "Ramping" detection rate as the default, it feels best suited for this application: getting caught at the center of their vision is very punishing, but getting caught at edge leaves you a lot of time to get out of the way.

The curve mode does not change during run time, so the values of the curve will be static, which allows them to be pre-calculated. To save from having to calculate the modifier and apply it to the MeterFillRate every time the player is in the vision cone, a lookup table is populated once, allowing O(1) access each time. At BeginPlay(), AngleFillCurveLUT is built with a 100 element array of floats, 1 element for each % of the (half) cone angle the player is within.

Arboriculture: Behavior

I am adding some complex, layered behavior, so to keep everything in order, the behavior states need the ability to handle differing "profiles" depending on the active action state.

For EnemyAgent to have varying behaviors based on conditions and actions, they now use an "Activity Profile".

note

Ignore the red squiggly underline, Visual Studio was being silly.

FEnemyActivityProfile

This is composed of three other structs:

  • FVisionModifier: Controls the size of the EnemyAgent's vision cone with length and width modifiers.
  • FVisionMeterProfile: Controls the rates of visual detection using fill and drain rate modifiers. FillRateModifier is applied to the calculated ratio of MaxFillRate currently active from the AngleFillCurveLUT calculation. This allows differing action states to hold different detection behaviors, for instance, an enemy that is actively investigating can spot the player quicker, and maintain their concentration for longer.
  • FSweepProfile: Establishes the behavior of a scan loop, assigning sweep count, size, speed and its own FVisionMeterProfile.

AI movement

Enemy AI Locomotion (Manual)

To make AI pathfinding, without the actor using pre-calculated movement paths from the engine, they need a solution routing around to objects blocking their path. The enemy is going to perform the following algorithm:

retarget vector diagram

  1. Set the movement target to the player's last location.
  2. Draw a straight line to the target.
  3. Is there a collision?
    • Get the location of the collision object's center
    • Calculate the vector from that center to the collision location
    • Increase the magnitude by a buffer amount
    • Set the endpoint of that vector as the new target destination.
  4. Move towards the target.

This is more expensive per-tick than a pre-calculated movement space, but for this stage of the project I am trying to avoid pre-built solutions and use as much pure code as I can.
